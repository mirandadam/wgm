# Good to use

This file is part of [the standard](STANDARD.md), and says what ["Good to use"](STANDARD.md#the-order) asks for in a product operated through a screen in a web browser. Each of the seven rules below is written from the moment someone would get stuck. Failing any one makes the product worse for everyone, but passing all seven does not show that it is good to use. Only observing the product in use shows that, as `WGM.md` says it is observed.

- **Every screen has its states.** The ideal, empty, loading, partial and error states are designed, not discovered, and wherever a phone is expected, offline is a sixth. Empty says what belongs here and offers the action that fills it. Loading shows a skeleton of what is coming, not the previous screen.
- **A failure is a sentence**, written once, at the boundary between the system and the person, in the product's words: what happened, why, and what to do next. It never contains the transport's words, a status code, a stack trace or an identifier the person has never seen, because they can do nothing with what they do not recognize. What the person entered is kept, and the sentence says where. A status says why, not only the status word ("Paused: payment overdue", not "Paused").
- **There is a budget for waiting.** A press visibly registers inside a tenth of a second, and the result shows inside four tenths. When it cannot, whatever loads shows its loading state by then, a submitted form keeps its busy state, and once a second has passed, an indicator says what is happening. Past ten seconds, there is a way to cancel or leave, or a recorded reason there is none. The tenth, the second and the ten seconds are the limits Card, Robertson and Mackinlay (1991) and Nielsen (1993) give for a response to feel instant, for the flow of thought to hold and for attention to stay, and four tenths is the Doherty threshold (Doherty and Thadhani, IBM, 1982). The budget is checked on the devices and network `WGM.md` lists, by a throttled test on every change or a hand measurement at a milestone, as `WGM.md` says.
- **The address is the state.** What survives a refresh and a shared link is decided and written down: the view, the thing open in it, and the choices a person made to get there. The back button works, and while a dialog or panel is open it closes that rather than leaving the page.
- **Recognition, not recall.** The context a decision needs (what was chosen, what it costs, what is left) is on the screen where the decision is made, not on the one before. An identifier someone would otherwise copy and type elsewhere is a link to what it names.
- **One vocabulary, chosen.** Labels use the words of the person, not the schema. The product's concepts and the one word for each are written with the product's other decisions, and where a retired word could return and no test would fail, a [vocabulary check](conventions/static-checks.md) catches it.
- **One control per job, and accessible.** The same thing looks the same everywhere and nothing else looks like it. Each screen has one primary action, which looks the same on every screen, and warnings and errors look the same wherever they appear. Buttons, status colors and text sizes come from a fixed set, as [conventions/static-checks.md](conventions/static-checks.md) describes. The conventions people already know (the products listed in `WGM.md`) are followed, not improved. A form is a form element and behaves like one: a label bound to every field, `autocomplete` and `inputmode`, submit on Enter, a busy state on submit, required fields marked before anyone types, and a number box that takes its limits from its own `min` and `max`. WCAG 2.2 AA is the minimum, not the goal, checked by a tool where one can and by a person for the rest, because tools find only part of what it asks. Each change a sighted person would notice is announced once to screen readers: an action that opens something or leads somewhere moves focus into it, and the dialog or page has a name that says where the person now is. A dialog that closes returns focus to what opened it, and any other change, such as a saved form, is announced by a live region. Targets are at least 44 CSS pixels where a finger is expected and 24 elsewhere, the sizes WCAG 2.2 sets at its AAA and AA levels for any pointer.
