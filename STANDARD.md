# The standard

This file says what "good" means: the ranked order every change is judged by, and what "Good to use" and "Easy to change" ask for in practice. Every change is held to it, whether or not anyone reviews it. What things are called, where they live and how decisions are written are in [conventions/](conventions/), and how a project shows that it meets "Trustworthy" is in [conventions/verification.md](conventions/verification.md).

The product may be software, or a text kept in a repository, such as a presentation, a report or an official document. A text is the product when its readers are those the work is for, not those who do it. Each fact it states, and each commitment it makes for someone, is a claim "Trustworthy" applies to, so it comes from a source you can point to, or it is a question, never a guess. Most of wgm was written for software, and its rules apply to such a text where their reasons do. Its checks for "Good to use" are those of [conventions/writing.md](conventions/writing.md) and [recipes/reviewing-documentation.md](recipes/reviewing-documentation.md), not the browser checks of [GOOD-TO-USE.md](GOOD-TO-USE.md).

A repository may keep a **`WGM.md`**, made from [template/TEMPLATE.md](template/TEMPLATE.md), to say where things are and what differs from wgm's defaults. Without one, or where it is silent, the defaults hold, and the rest is inferred from the repository, saying which facts were inferred. **Whoever asked** is the person you work for, which in a session is the person at the terminal. Where nobody can answer, as in a scheduled run, the question goes in the report and nothing that depends on it is done.

## The order

1. **Trustworthy.** The product does what it claims, and can show it. It states what it does not do. It keeps working, and when it fails it never loses anything without saying so. Nobody sees or changes what they are not allowed to.
2. **Good to use.** Measured by how rarely people get stuck and how easily they get unstuck, not by how much it offers.
3. **Easy to change.** Measured by how much the next person must understand, and how long the work takes them once they do, not by how many lines there are. This comes before running and building it because a product that is easy to change can be made cheaper later, and a cheap product that is hard to change can be made easy to change later only at a far greater cost.
4. **Cheap to run.** In money, and in the attention of the people who keep it running.
5. **Cheap to build.** What the task at hand costs to make, now. It is last because it is paid once, and a shortfall in any item above it is paid for as long as the product lives. So when a requirement in hand can be met by a design that is cheaper now or by one that is cheaper over the product's life, the second wins by default.

Whoever asked decides what the product lets people do. This standard is about giving them that at the least human time and money over its life. "Trustworthy" is a condition, never traded. The other four are costs, ordered by who pays them longest and most often: the people who use the product, the next person to change it, those who run it, and whoever builds it now. Nobody can measure these costs while making a change, so the order stands in for the measurement.

A change is judged by what it does to each item, in this order, and the order is the only ranking. Every rule in wgm serves an item of it, a finding weighs as much as the item it harms, and when two rules pull apart, the one serving the earlier item wins. Each rule says why it exists, and applies where its reason does. Where the reason does not hold, neither does the rule, and you say so when you set it aside, in the report or the commit message, rather than skipping it in silence. A rule that gives no reason applies as written. A number a rule sets is a default, because a number that suits one project rarely suits every other, and `WGM.md` may set another.

**Ask before** you make an earlier item of the order worse to improve a later one, set aside a rule that serves "Trustworthy", choose between two parts of "Trustworthy" that pull apart, change a written decision, or make a choice that [needs an answer](conventions/decisions.md#what-needs-an-answer). Say what it costs. If whoever asked still wants it, that is the answer: write it as [conventions/decisions.md](conventions/decisions.md) says, and go on. A written decision that seems wrong is changed this way, never worked around, because a workaround leaves written a decision that is no longer true. When two parts of any other item pull apart, you choose and write the trade down. Findings in what someone else answers for, such as a colleague's change or passage, or a structure an outside authority sets, are reported, and the thing itself is changed only when whoever asked says so.

Being earlier in the order does not justify any cost. An improvement there must still be worth what it costs in later items. You judge that, and write it down where the next person to pay the cost will read it, such as the [as-built reference](conventions/documents.md) for a cost paid each time the product is run or changed, a comment for one the next editor of that code pays, or the commit message for one paid once. A cost that repeats belongs to the item where it repeats: a slow test suite is paid on every change, so it is a cost of changing the product.

## What good to use asks for

For a product operated through a screen in a web browser, [GOOD-TO-USE.md](GOOD-TO-USE.md) gives seven rules, each written from the moment someone would get stuck.

## What easy to change asks for

- **Lean towards simplicity.** Simplicity means fewer concepts, fewer special cases and fewer modes, not fewer lines. When two designs both work, take the one that needs less explaining. Build for the second case when it arrives, not before: no configuration that nothing reads and no hook without a caller, because a case nobody has asked for is not a requirement in hand. Delete commented-out code, since git keeps it. Do not handle inputs that the type system or the caller's contract already excludes, and if unsure, assert, but check input that crosses a trust boundary where it enters, because there the contract is only a claim, and "Trustworthy" comes first. [recipes/reviewing-the-mechanisms.md](recipes/reviewing-the-mechanisms.md) is the long form.
- **Prefer the fix that makes the next mistake harder.** The unit of a fix is the cause, not the symptom in front of you, because if the same mistake can happen in three other places, repairing one is not finished work. Where something holds only by luck or by convention, make it hold by construction, because a comment asking the next person not to break it is not a rule. Generalize the shape, not the feature set: a name and a signature that carry the concept rather than the first caller's needs, and documentation written as part of the design rather than after it. A layer, an option or an abstraction still needs a current requirement behind it, so inline what has one concrete user until a second exists.
- **Every behavior is decided or incidental.** Every behavior costs whoever maintains it: code to read, tests to keep, and a place for defects. A behavior is **decided** when whoever asked decided it and it is written where [conventions/decisions.md](conventions/decisions.md) says. It is **incidental** otherwise, a choice made while building, which is not a requirement because it exists, because it is old, or because a document describes it. Before altering or removing an incidental behavior, you look for what relies on it, in the code and in what is known of how people use the product, because a person who relies on it ranks above what the removal saves. You go ahead when nothing is found, unless the choice [needs an answer](conventions/decisions.md#what-needs-an-answer).
- **Do now what belongs to now.** Work that is part of the task at hand gets done as part of it. Before writing anything down as "later", ask whether it can simply be done now, and if it cannot, put it where it will be most useful, because much of what reads like backlog is a sentence that belongs beside the code it describes.
- **Write for a person.** Everything a person reads (code, comments, tests, commit messages, documents, failure output, the account of the work) is written to be understood without holding the whole codebase in mind. Put the explanation where the reader will be standing when they need it, say why before what (a file and line number is a reference, not an explanation), make failure messages say what to do next, and name things so that the name carries the meaning. Write as [conventions/writing.md](conventions/writing.md) says, and commit messages as [conventions/commits.md](conventions/commits.md) says. In code, comments and tests, remove anything whose absence would change neither what the product does nor what a reader takes from the text, at the statement, then the section, then the whole file, because everything kept is read again by every next person. Cut ideas, not words, and keep an example only where a reader would get the rule wrong without it.

## Checking your own work

Before you call something done, ask yourself:

1. Is there a design that also works and needs less explaining?
2. Have you changed what made this possible, or only what went wrong this time?
3. Is anything on your "later" list actually doable now?
4. Could someone who does not know this code read what you wrote and act on it?
5. Did this make an earlier item of the order worse without asking? If so, ask before calling it done.
6. Is every decision taken for this work written [where decisions are written](conventions/decisions.md#where-decisions-are-written)?
